Google Ads for Medical Spas: Fill Your Booking Calendar Without Getting Disapproved

Medical aesthetics has the highest ad disapproval rate of any healthcare category on Google. Here's why — and how to run campaigns that stay live, reach buyers, and convert to bookings.

📅 July 2026 ⏱ 8 min read ✍ Stickercast

If you run a medical spa, you've probably already discovered that Google Ads is not as straightforward as it looks. You set up a campaign, write headlines mentioning Botox or fillers, and within days you're staring at a wall of disapproved ads, APPROVED_LIMITED flags, and policy notifications that read like they were written by a lawyer for other lawyers.

You're not doing anything wrong. Medical aesthetics sits at the intersection of Google's healthcare ad policy, its prescription drug restrictions, and its personalised advertising rules — three separate policy layers that interact in ways that catch most medspa owners completely off guard.

This post explains exactly what's happening, why, and how to build Google Ads campaigns for a medical spa that stay approved, reach people who are actually ready to book, and convert at a rate worth the spend.

Why Medical Spa Ads Are Uniquely Difficult

Most businesses can describe their services in plain language and run ads without incident. Medical spas can't — because several of the most common treatment names are classified by Google as either prescription drugs, restricted healthcare products, or both.

  • Botox — classified as a prescription drug (botulinum toxin). Ads mentioning it by name trigger an automatic policy review and typically receive APPROVED_LIMITED status, meaning they serve in fewer placements at higher cost.
  • Dysport, Xeomin, Nuceiva — same category as Botox. All neuromodulators are flagged the same way.
  • Juvederm, Restylane, Sculptra — dermal fillers are classified as medical devices or prescription products depending on the country. Same result.
  • Before/after claims — Google restricts ads that imply guaranteed outcomes from medical procedures. "Look 10 years younger" or "eliminate your wrinkles" triggers a separate review under misleading claims policy.
  • Personalised healthcare ads — Google restricts targeting based on health conditions. This limits audience layering options that work fine for other businesses.

None of this means you can't advertise. It means you need to understand the policy layer before you build the campaign, not after your ads get flagged.

APPROVED vs APPROVED_LIMITED: What It Actually Means

When Google reviews a medical aesthetics ad, it can come back in one of three states:

StatusWhat It MeansImpact on Your Campaign
APPROVED Ad serves normally across all eligible placements Full reach, no restrictions
APPROVED_LIMITED Ad serves, but excluded from some placements and audiences Reduced reach, typically higher effective CPC
DISAPPROVED Ad does not serve at all Zero impressions until fixed

Most medspa owners who use drug brand names in their headlines end up with APPROVED_LIMITED rather than outright DISAPPROVED — which is why the problem is easy to miss. The ads are technically running, but at a fraction of their potential reach and at inflated cost. You're paying more for less.

⚠️ Common mistake: Using "Botox" in a headline, seeing the ad go live, and assuming everything is fine. An APPROVED_LIMITED ad can run for months while quietly underperforming. Most medspa owners never check the policy status column in Google Ads — it's not visible by default.

What Language Actually Works

The solution isn't to pretend you don't offer Botox. It's to understand where in the ad structure each type of language belongs.

Ad ElementUse ThisAvoid This
Headlines Injectable treatments, neuromodulators, wrinkle relaxers, filler consultation, aesthetics appointment Botox, Dysport, Juvederm, Restylane (by brand name)
Descriptions Natural-looking results, licensed injectors, medical-grade treatments, book a consultation "Guaranteed results", "safe and risk-free", before/after claims
Sitelinks Can reference specific treatments more directly — "Botox Consultation", "Filler Pricing" Still avoid unqualified outcome claims
Landing page Full treatment detail, specific products, pricing — policy is less strict on landing pages Misleading before/after galleries, unverified testimonials

The underlying logic: Google's automated review scans headline text most aggressively. The same word that flags a headline often passes in a sitelink or description with less scrutiny. Understanding this hierarchy lets you communicate your full treatment menu without triggering the policy filter on your primary ad text.

How to Structure a Medspa Campaign That Converts

The biggest campaign structure mistake medical spas make is building one broad campaign with a handful of generic keywords like "medical spa near me" and "aesthetics clinic." That's not wrong — it's just not where the money is.

The searches that convert to bookings are specific. Someone typing "Botox consultation [your city]" is in a completely different buying position than someone typing "what is Botox." Both are searching for the same product; only one is ready to book.

Recommended ad group structure

Ad GroupTarget KeywordsIntent
Injectables — booking botox consultation [city], filler appointment near me, injectable treatment booking Ready to book
Injectables — research botox cost [city], how much does filler cost, best injector [city] Comparing options
Laser treatments laser hair removal [city], laser skin resurfacing near me, IPL treatment [city] Mixed
Skin & facials medical facial near me, hydrafacial [city], chemical peel appointment Mixed
Body contouring body contouring [city], fat reduction treatment near me, coolsculpting alternative Research to booking
Brand / clinic name [Your clinic name] + variations High intent — protect your brand

Separating booking intent from research intent matters because you want to bid more aggressively on booking searches and more conservatively on research searches. Lumping them together means you're either overpaying for clicks that rarely convert, or underbidding for the clicks that always do.

Negative Keywords: The Medspa Edition

Negative keywords are what stop your budget from being wasted on searches that will never book a consultation. Medical spas have a very specific set of searches to exclude — most of which are invisible until you're already paying for them.

  • DIY and at-home — "at home botox", "DIY filler", "botox kit", "home microneedling"
  • Training and certification — "botox training", "filler course", "aesthetics certification", "how to inject"
  • Medical information — "botox side effects", "filler complications", "botox allergy", "is botox safe"
  • Discount and deal-seeking — "groupon botox", "cheap filler", "discount botox near me"
  • Competitor brands — add specific competitor clinic names in your area
  • Job searches — "medspa jobs", "injector position", "aesthetics nurse hiring"
  • Social media — "facebook ads for medspa", "instagram medspa marketing" (people researching marketing, not booking treatments)

📊 Industry insight: In a typical unmanaged medspa campaign, 35–50% of clicks come from searches that will never book a treatment — information queries, training searches, and deal-hunters. Negative keywords applied at campaign launch eliminate most of this before you spend a single dollar on it.

Provincial and College Standards for Medspa Advertising

Beyond Google's own policies, medical spa advertising in Canada is also subject to provincial standards — and these vary by treatment and provider type.

  • Physician-injectors are subject to their provincial college's advertising standards (CPSA in Alberta, CPSO in Ontario, CPSBC in BC). These generally prohibit testimonials that imply guaranteed results and require any advertised claims to be evidence-based.
  • Nurse injectors (RNs/NPs) are governed by their respective nursing college standards, which have similar restrictions on outcome claims.
  • Aesthetic treatments without a regulated practitioner (cosmetic-only spas) have fewer college restrictions, but are still bound by consumer protection rules against misleading advertising.

In practice, this means any ad copy that says "eliminate wrinkles in one session," "guaranteed results," or "the best injector in [city]" carries compliance risk — regardless of what Google's policy says. What Good Performance Looks Like for a Medspa

The metrics that matter for a medspa are different from a walk-in clinic. You're not tracking patient walk-ins — you're tracking consultation bookings, and then the conversion from consultation to treatment.

MetricMedspa BenchmarkWhat to Watch
Click-through rate (CTR) 4–8% for well-targeted aesthetics ads Below 3% = ad copy needs work
Cost per consultation inquiry $25–60 for booking-intent keywords Depends heavily on avg. treatment value
Consultation-to-treatment rate 60–80% (off the ads — these are warm leads) Track offline; compare to walk-in rate
Return on ad spend (ROAS) 5:1 to 12:1 for high-ticket treatments Filler/Botox packages $300–800 make CPL economics strong

The economics work well for medical spas because the average treatment value is high. Even at $50 per consultation inquiry, if 70% of those consultations convert to a $350 Botox appointment, the return on ad spend is strong. The challenge is tracking it — most medspa booking systems don't automatically connect a Google click to a treatment booking. Setting up proper conversion tracking before launch is non-negotiable.

The Bottom Line

Medical spa Google Ads can fill your booking calendar — but they require a different approach than most other businesses. The combination of prescription drug classifications, before/after claim restrictions, and provincial professional standards means that generic campaigns built without this knowledge will either get flagged, underperform, or quietly drain your budget on the wrong searches.

The medspas that do well on Google Ads are the ones that treat campaign structure as a compliance exercise first and a marketing exercise second. Get the policy layer right, target booking intent specifically, exclude the searches that will never convert, and the economics follow.
