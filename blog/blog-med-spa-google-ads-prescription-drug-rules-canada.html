Why your med spa's Google Ads keep getting disapproved, and how to advertise injectables in Canada anyway

Stickercast · October 2026 · 7 min read

Short answer: Neurotoxins like Botox are prescription drugs. Google only lets certified advertisers use prescription-drug terms in keywords, ads or the pages ads link to, and Health Canada limits public advertising of prescription drugs to name, price and quantity. Clinics can still advertise the service: build campaigns around the treatment and the concern (forehead lines, crow's feet), keep drug brand names off ads and ad landing pages, and avoid benefit claims about the drug.

It's one of the most common frustrations we hear from clinic owners: the ad for your most popular treatment is the one Google won't run. Sometimes it's disapproved within minutes. Sometimes it runs for a week and then quietly stops. Either way, it's almost always the same cause, and it's fixable.

Two separate sets of rules apply

Canadian med spas advertising injectables deal with two rulebooks at once: Google's advertising policies, and federal law on advertising prescription drugs. They overlap, but they're not the same thing.

1. Google's prescription drug policy

Google restricts ads that promote prescription drugs. According to Google's prescription drug services policy, advertisers must be approved before they can bid on keywords containing prescription-drug terms, and Google says it "errs on the side of caution", especially for landing pages that appear to promote prescription medicines.

The certification categories are built for online pharmacies, telemedicine providers and pharmaceutical manufacturers. A med spa usually doesn't fit any of them. In practice that means three things get flagged:

  • Keywords that contain a prescription drug name, such as a neurotoxin brand.
  • Ad text that mentions the drug name.
  • The landing page the ad sends people to, if it features the drug name. This catches clinics out: the ad itself can be clean and still get disapproved because of the page.

Healthcare marketing platform DoctorLogic, for example, tells its clients it cannot run ads, bid on keywords or link to landing pages containing restricted prescription terms without the right verification. Their note is written for US practices, but it describes the same Google behaviour Canadian clinics run into.

2. Health Canada's rules on advertising prescription drugs

Separately, the Food and Drug Regulations limit advertising of prescription drugs to the public. Health Canada's guidance says this advertising can't go beyond the drug's name, price and quantity, and it uses Botox as its example of a prescription drug clinics sometimes market to consumers: no direct or indirect reference can be made to its therapeutic use or benefits.

Health Canada lists the issues it commonly sees with clinic marketing of this product:

  • videos describing the procedure
  • before-and-after treatment pictures
  • pictures alluding to what the drug is used for
  • testimonials about its therapeutic benefits

Health Canada also notes that the Food and Drugs Act doesn't apply to advertising of services. That distinction is what makes compliant advertising possible: you can promote your clinic, your injectors and your treatment service, as long as you aren't promoting a specific prescription drug's benefits. Provinces and professional colleges may add their own rules on top.

A practical setup that keeps ads running

  1. Build a separate ad landing page. Your main website can carry more detail, but the page your ads point to should be clean: treatment, concerns, your team, how a consultation works, and a booking form.
  2. Target the concern, not the product. People search “how to get rid of forehead lines” or “11 lines treatment”. These searches are often cheaper and show clear intent.
  3. Lead with a consultation. It suits a medical treatment, sets expectations properly and stays clear of drug-benefit claims.
  4. Check the Policy Manager weekly. A disapproved ad stops spending without any alert you'll notice. Look at ad status, not only performance.
  5. Fix and resubmit rather than repeatedly appealing. If an ad or page genuinely contains restricted terms, appeals won't help. Edit the ad or swap the landing page, and Google re-reviews it automatically.

What about dermal fillers?

Hyaluronic acid fillers are generally regulated as medical devices in Canada rather than prescription drugs, so the specific prescription-drug restrictions above don't apply in the same way. Filler ads still go through Google's healthcare review, and misleading or guaranteed-results claims are a common reason for disapproval across all aesthetic treatments.

Common questions

Can a med spa in Canada advertise Botox on Google?

Not by name without Google's prescription-drug certification, which is designed for pharmacies, telemedicine providers and manufacturers. Clinics can advertise the treatment service using concern-based keywords and ad text that leaves out the drug brand name.

Why was my ad disapproved when it didn't mention the drug?

Google reviews the landing page as well as the ad. If the page the ad links to features a prescription-drug name, the ad can be disapproved even when the ad text is clean.

Does Health Canada allow before-and-after photos for anti-wrinkle injections?

Health Canada lists before-and-after pictures among the common issues it sees in clinic marketing of this prescription drug. Check with your regulator or a lawyer before using them.

Is this legal advice?

No. This is a general overview of Google's policies and Health Canada's published guidance. Speak to a lawyer or your professional college about your specific situation.
